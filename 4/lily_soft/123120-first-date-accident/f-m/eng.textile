First Date Accident

Author: lily_soft

*000*2026-04-06 16:47

David had re-read Sarah's last message four times on the bus ride over. *Can't wait to finally meet you :)* Five months of texting, voice notes past midnight, laughing at the same obscure references, and now a park on a bright September afternoon felt almost too real.

He was twenty-six years old and his palms were sweating.

He spotted her before she saw him. She was standing near the iron gate at the park entrance, phone tilted down in her hand, wearing a yellow sundress that caught the afternoon light perfectly. Brown hair in a loose braid, one sandal tapping idly against the pavement. She looked exactly like her photos, only warmer. More present.

"David?" She looked up and smiled, and the smile reached her eyes immediately.

"That's me," he said, then immediately wanted to walk backward into traffic.

She laughed. "Good. I was a little worried you'd turn out to be a catfish."

"Still could be," he offered. "Give it twenty minutes."

The first twenty minutes stretched into two hours without either of them noticing. They walked the long path around the park's duck pond, stopped to watch a pair of mallards bicker furiously over a crust of bread, sat on a weathered bench and talked about their worst jobs. Sarah had worked a summer at a smoothie chain and once blended an entire cup of protein powder into someone's car through an open window. David had done a stint answering phones for a call centre and, on his third day, accidentally transferred a caller directly to the CEO while trying to put them on hold.

She laughed at that one hard enough that she had to put her hand on his arm to steady herself.

It was easy. That was the word he kept coming back to. Easier than he'd feared, easier than most things had felt in a long time. She had a way of talking that made him feel like whatever he said next would be exactly right.

He noticed, somewhere around the ninety-minute mark, that he needed the bathroom. Not urgently. A background pressure, the kind you note and file away for later. There were public restrooms on the far side of the park but they were drifting the other direction, and Sarah had just started a story about her flatmate's ferret, and it felt wrong to interrupt. He crossed his legs for a moment where he sat, then uncrossed them. Fine.

By the time they reached the rose garden the pressure had become more persistent. He shifted his weight from one foot to the other without thinking about it.

"You alright?" Sarah asked, watching him sidelong.

"Yeah, totally. I might need a bathroom at some point though."

"There's one back near the entrance," she said. "We can head that way."

"It's fine," he said.

He was wrong.

They were walking along a narrow gravel path between two tall hedgerows when his body simply gave up on him. There was no dramatic warning, no final urgent signal he could act on. One moment he was walking, the next warmth was flooding through him with a completeness that left him frozen mid-step. He stopped walking. Stopping didn't help. He stood completely still while it happened, the heat spreading down both thighs, seeping through the denim of his jeans, pooling briefly at his feet before the gravel absorbed it. The sound was very soft and very awful. It seemed to go on for a long time.

When it finally stopped he could not move.

"David?" Sarah had taken a few steps ahead before she realized he wasn't beside her anymore. She turned around. Her eyes dropped to his jeans, taking in the spreading dark stain across his crotch, down both legs, the small wet patches soaking into the gravel at his feet. Her mouth opened slightly.

The heat in his face arrived all at once, climbing from his collar to his hairline. "I -- I don't --" He had no sentence. There was nothing to say that made this better.

A short laugh escaped her, involuntary and quickly smothered behind her hand. Her eyes were wide. "Oh my God," she said softly.

"I'm so sorry." His voice came out thin and strange. He was twenty-six years old and he had just wet himself completely in a public park in front of a woman he'd been hoping to kiss, and he genuinely could not think of a single thing to do with his body.

"David." She stepped closer, still visibly trying to get her expression under control, the ghost of a smile still pulling at the corners of her mouth. She looked him over slowly, taking in the full extent of it, and the brief silence while she did this was the longest of his life. "Are you okay? Are you hurt?"

"I'm fine. I have no idea how that happened."

"Okay." She looked at him steadily, her amusement settling into something more decisive. "My place is ten minutes from here. Come on."

"Sarah, I can't just walk through a park like this."

"You're already like this," she pointed out. Then she reached out and took his hand.

It made everything worse and somehow also better at the same time. They walked, her thumb lightly over his knuckles, him acutely aware of how he looked and smelled and sounded with each step. The wet denim clung to his legs. A few people they passed glanced at him and looked away quickly. One older man stared openly for a moment before his wife tugged at his sleeve.

They were nearly at the park gate when Mia appeared.

David didn't know Mia. He would have preferred to go his entire life not meeting her for the first time in this particular moment. She was jogging toward the park entrance, somewhere in her mid-twenties, short dark hair, athletic and self-assured in a way that radiated complete comfort in her own skin. She pulled out an earbud when she spotted Sarah and raised a hand.

"Hey! I thought you had your big date today."

"I do," Sarah said, and the small smile came back.

Mia looked at David. Then her gaze traveled downward, unhurried, taking in the state of his jeans from waist to knee. Her hand came up over her mouth. The sound she made was not quite a cough.

"Oh wow," she said. Her eyes came back up to his face, slowly, cataloguing his expression along the way. David was fairly certain he had achieved a shade of red that had no common name. "How old are you, exactly?"

"Mia," Sarah said.

"I'm asking genuinely!" She was grinning now, not pretending otherwise. "Like, what happened? Did you just --" She gestured vaguely at his lower half, still grinning.

"We're going to my place," Sarah said, and tugged David gently forward.

"It was great meeting you!" Mia called after them. He could hear her still laughing, trying to muffle it and not quite managing, until they turned the corner onto the street.

He didn't speak for most of the walk. Sarah didn't push him. She kept his hand and talked lightly about the neighborhood, pointed out a bakery she liked, mentioned a cat that was always sitting in the same window of a flat above a laundry. Filling the quiet without requiring anything from him.

Her building was a narrow brick place on a quiet street. She buzzed them in and they climbed to the second floor. She had just fit her key into the lock when the door at the far end of the hall swung open and a woman stepped out carrying a bag of recycling.

Sarah's mother. David understood it immediately, the resemblance was plain. Same face, same straight posture, a few inches shorter than Sarah. She was somewhere in her early fifties, dark hair going silver at the temples, wearing an apron with a pattern of small blue flowers. She stopped when she saw them.

"You must be David. I'm Carol." Her eyes moved to his jeans. He watched her register what she was seeing. She did not laugh. She pressed her lips together in a way that was, somehow, more thorough than laughing. A long, slow breath through her nose. "Oh, sweetheart," she said, her voice gentle and faintly amused. "You couldn't hold it till you got here?"

"Mum," Sarah said.

"I'm not saying a word." Carol was already moving. "Come inside, both of you. Don't stand in the hall. Sarah, get him a towel. Those clothes need washing."

The shower was small and clean and smelled faintly of something floral. David stood under the hot water for longer than strictly necessary, staring at the tiles, trying to locate a version of himself that wasn't completely mortified. He found a partial one after a while. Good enough. He turned off the taps, dried off with the thick blue towel Sarah had left on the rail, wrapped it around his waist, and opened the bathroom door.

His jeans and underwear were gone. He had a blurry memory of Sarah saying something through the door about leaving them out, but it registered fully now: he was standing in a stranger's hallway in a borrowed towel. From down the hall he could hear the washing machine starting its cycle.

"Sarah?" he called.

She appeared from the kitchen holding two mugs. She had changed into dark leggings and an oversized rust-colored jumper, feet bare on the hardwood. She looked entirely unrattled by everything.

"Your clothes will be about an hour. Tea?"

He sat at the kitchen table in the towel. Sarah sat across from him. He wrapped his hands around the mug and let the warmth seep in. His face had gone hot again. He kept his eyes on the tea, turned the mug slowly in his hands. But Sarah was not acting as though anything dramatic had happened, and there was something steadying about the way she looked at him, direct and calm, the same way she'd looked at him all afternoon.

Then Carol appeared in the kitchen doorway.

She was holding something in her hand. White, folded, with a faint crinkle to it. David's brain processed the image slowly, then all at once.

"Sarah mentioned it's not the first time you've had trouble," Carol said, with the matter-of-fact ease of someone who had stopped being embarrassed by bodily functions a long time ago. "My nephew wore these well into his thirties. No shame in it. And you can't sit around wrapped in a towel for an hour." She set the folded adult diaper on the kitchen table between them.

David stared at it. Then at Carol. Then at Sarah, who was watching him with steady, patient eyes.

The tips of his ears went scarlet. He looked back down at the diaper on the table. He was aware that Mia was out there somewhere, probably texting people. He was aware that Carol was watching him the way a person watches someone work through something at their own pace.

"Okay," he said.

Carol took him back to the bathroom with no ceremony, talked him through the tapes with the practicality of someone discussing how to change a fuse, and left him to it. The diaper was thick between his legs, and when he walked it made a faint, unmistakable crinkle with each step. He stood in front of the mirror for a moment, just him and the towel still loose around his waist and the diaper visible below the hem. He looked at his own face. Still the same face. Still faintly red around the ears but roughly the same person.

He went back to the kitchen. Sarah handed him more tea without comment.

After a while they moved to the living room. Carol put on a film, something warm and a little silly, and the three of them settled onto the sofa. David sat between Sarah and the armrest, the towel across his lap, the diaper crackling quietly whenever he shifted position. Sarah had her feet tucked underneath her and was leaning slightly against his shoulder. Carol was on the far end with a glass of wine, occasionally commenting on the film.

It was, against all reasonable odds, comfortable.

About forty minutes in, David noticed the familiar pressure building again. Softer this time, more distant. He was warm from the tea and from Sarah's shoulder against his, and he was watching something genuinely funny happen on screen and Sarah laughed and her whole side pressed against him, and the pressure arrived and then passed before he had properly decided what to do about it. Warmth spread through the padding, contained, quiet, invisible to anyone watching.

He sat very still.

Sarah glanced over at him. Something in his expression must have shifted because she looked at him for a moment, measured and knowing, and then she reached over and pressed her hand over his once, a brief, firm squeeze, and looked back at the screen.

He sat in the warmth and watched the film. The machine beeped from down the hall near the end of the second act. The September light through the window went from gold to amber to the flat grey of early evening.

His jeans came back from the dryer a little wrinkled and still faintly warm from the drum. Sarah handed them to him folded and didn't make a production of it. He took them to the bathroom and changed back. He stood in front of the mirror again for a moment, taking stock. Man who had wet his pants in a park. Been laughed at by a stranger. Had a diaper put on him by his date's mother. Wet that diaper too, during a film, on a sofa. He looked more or less normal. A little pink still. But normal.

Sarah was waiting by the front door when he came out, jacket over her arm.

"I'll walk you to the bus," she said.

Outside, the air had gone cool and smelled like the end of summer. They walked close together, not quite holding hands but almost. She kept bumping her shoulder gently into his as they walked, not saying much. At the bus stop she turned to face him properly, and there was something in her expression that was warm and amused and something else he couldn't name yet.

"So," she said.

"So," he said.

"Next week?" she asked. Just like that. No preamble, no careful hedging.

He looked at her. She was watching him steadily, one hand in her jumper pocket, the streetlight catching the edge of her braid. He thought about the afternoon. The duck pond, the ferret story, the way she had taken his hand in the park without hesitating, the way she had squeezed his hand on the sofa. He thought about Mia laughing, and Carol at the table with that dry, knowing expression, and how he had felt sitting there in a towel at a stranger's kitchen table and somehow found it survivable.

"Yeah," he said. "Next week."

The bus pulled up. He got on and found a window seat, and through the glass he could see her still standing at the stop, hands easy at her sides, watching the bus pull away. She raised one hand in a small wave. He watched her until the street bent and she disappeared, and then he sat back and looked at his own hands resting in his lap.

He was, despite everything, smiling. Genuinely and without reservation, the way he hadn't smiled on a bus in longer than he could remember.

His phone buzzed. Sarah.

*Next Saturday. I know a better park.*

He stared at the message for a moment. Then he typed back: *I'll find a bathroom first this time.*

Three dots appeared, then: *No promises needed :)*

He put his phone in his pocket and watched the city pass by outside the window, the lights coming on one by one as the sky darkened, and thought that this had been, measurably and against every expectation, one of the better days he'd had in a long time.
